Frameworks and call-offs explained for construction firms

A large share of public-sector building work never appears as an open tender, because it goes to firms already on a framework. Knowing how frameworks work tells you which opportunities you can bid for now and which you need to plan for.

Checked against GOV.UK guidance and the Procurement Act 2023 on 16 November 2026. This is general information, not legal advice.

What a framework is

A framework is an agreement between a public body and one or more suppliers that sets the terms for contracts the body may award later. The firms on it are chosen through a competitive process. Once it is in place, individual jobs, called call-off contracts, are awarded to firms on the framework without advertising each job publicly.

Frameworks are common for responsive repairs, planned maintenance, roofing, mechanical and electrical work, and minor building works. Some are set up by one council or housing association; others are run by purchasing organisations and used by many public bodies.

How long frameworks last

Under the Procurement Act 2023, which applies to procurements started from 24 February 2025 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, a framework can generally last up to four years. Utilities and defence and security frameworks can last up to eight. A buyer can set a longer term if the nature of the work requires it; government guidance gives large construction and infrastructure programmes as an example.

The Act also introduced open frameworks: a series of frameworks on substantially the same terms that lets new suppliers join at set points, over a total of up to eight years. For smaller firms this matters, because a framework is no longer always closed to newcomers for its whole life.

How call-offs are awarded

Call-offs are normally awarded through a competitive selection process among the firms on the framework, often called a mini-competition. A framework can allow direct award without further competition if it sets out the core terms of the call-off contract and an objective way of choosing the supplier.

What this means when you see a tender

  • A tender to set up a framework is often your one chance in four years to get on to that buyer's list for that type of work. It can be worth bidding even if the first year's work is uncertain.
  • Repeated awards to the same firms usually mean a framework is in place. Noticehawk flags this in a tender's buyer history, for example "mostly as call-offs from a framework".
  • Lots split a framework by type of work, value band or area. Smaller firms often do best on a single local or lower-value lot.
  • Planned procurement notices can give advance warning that a framework is about to be re-procured.

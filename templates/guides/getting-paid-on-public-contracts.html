Getting paid on public contracts: payment terms and late payment

Public buyers are expected to pay quickly, and the law now backs that up with a 30-day payment term in most public contracts. This guide explains what that term says, what makes an invoice count, and what you can do when a payment is late. It is written for small construction and maintenance firms, whether you contract directly with a buyer or work as a subcontractor.

Checked against Cabinet Office Procurement Act 2023 guidance on electronic invoicing and payment, the prompt payment policy and GOV.UK late payment guidance on 24 November 2026. This is general information, not legal advice.

The 30-day rule

Under the Procurement Act 2023, most public contracts carry an implied term that the buyer pays a valid invoice within 30 days of receiving it. If your invoice shows a later due date, the buyer can pay by that date instead. The term only applies if the invoice is valid and not disputed.

Day 1 is the day the buyer receives your invoice, and payment must be made before the end of day 30. If the buyer uses an electronic invoicing system, receipt is the day the invoice reaches that system. Payment counts when you receive it, although delays the buyer is not responsible for, such as a payment system outage, are disregarded.

A contract cannot replace the 30 days with a longer period. A shorter period can be agreed. The term is not implied into concession contracts, private utility contracts or school contracts, although a buyer may choose to include it.

What makes an invoice valid

An invoice is valid if it is in the required electronic form, or if it meets any other requirement in your contract and contains the minimum information. That minimum is:

  • the name of the party sending the invoice
  • a description of the goods, services or works
  • the sum requested
  • a unique identification number

The guidance does not define the word invoice. A timesheet can count if it carries that minimum information. Read the contract for the invoice address or email, any named contact, and the dispute procedure, and follow them exactly. Invoices sent to the wrong place are the easiest ones to lose.

Buyers must also accept electronic invoices in the required structured format, and can ask you to use their invoicing system. Other methods, such as post, can be agreed, but you always keep the right to send an electronic invoice that gets processed.

If the buyer disputes or rejects an invoice

The 30 days stop applying if the buyer considers an invoice invalid or disputes it. The buyer must tell you without undue delay. So the first thing to do when an invoice goes quiet is check whether you have had a rejection or query notice, and answer it quickly. Fix a missing purchase order number or description and resubmit, keeping a record of the date you did so.

If payment is made in part, the guidance treats the invoice as unpaid until you accept the part payment as full payment. Do not agree to that in writing unless you mean it.

Paying your own subcontractors

The same 30-day term is implied into most public subcontracts, meaning contracts to carry out all or part of a public contract. If you are the main contractor, you must pay a subcontractor within 30 days of receiving their invoice, or by their later due date if it falls after the 30 days. If you are the subcontractor, you can ask the main contractor to confirm in writing that your contract carries the term before you accept the work.

The term is not implied into subcontracts of concession contracts, private utility contracts or school contracts. Separately, for central government contracts worth more than £5 million a year, Procurement Policy Note 021 has departments carry out spot checks on payment down the supply chain, applying from 1 October 2025.

Smaller contracts

The Act also implies the payment terms into regulated below-threshold contracts and their relevant subcontracts. Many small firms work on contracts of that size, so do not assume that a low-value job sits outside the rule. Check the contract terms for the payment period either way.

What buyers publish about how fast they pay

Buyers covered by the Act must publish a payments compliance notice every six months on the central digital platform. It shows the average number of days they took to pay, the share of invoices paid within 1 to 30, 31 to 60 and 61 days or more, the share paid in line with the 30-day term, and the share that were due but not paid. Those figures are worth a look before you bid, because they show how a buyer behaves in practice. Government guidance says central government buyers aim to pay 100% of valid, undisputed invoices within 30 days.

When a payment is late

  1. Check the invoice. Confirm it was valid, sent to the right place and not disputed. Note the date it was received.
  2. Chase in writing. Quote the invoice number, the date received and the 30-day term. Keep copies.
  3. Claim statutory interest. Under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998, you can charge 8% plus the Bank of England base rate on a late business payment. A lower rate cannot be used where the contract is with a public authority. Interest is not paid automatically, so you have to ask for it.
  4. Claim the fixed debt recovery sum. It is £40 for a debt up to £999.99, £70 for £1,000 to £9,999.99 and £100 for £10,000 or more, per late payment. You can also claim reasonable costs for each attempt to recover the debt.
  5. Complain. The Cabinet Office Public Procurement Review Service investigates concerns about payment on a public contract, including complaints about a main contractor that is not paying its supply chain. You can email publicprocurementreview@cabinetoffice.gov.uk.

Construction contracts can also carry their own statutory payment rights. The official pages used for this guide do not cover them, so read the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996 or take advice before relying on a particular notice or deadline.

Protect your cash flow before you sign

  • Find the payment clause and the invoicing instructions in the tender documents, and ask a clarification question if they are unclear.
  • Note whether you invoice monthly, at stages or on completion, and build that into your cash flow before you price.
  • Send invoices as soon as the work is certified, and keep proof of when they were sent.
  • Pay your own subcontractors on time, as central government buyers can spot check it on larger contracts.

The paperwork buyers ask for: a checklist for construction tenders

Most public-sector construction tenders ask for the same core documents. Firms that have them ready can spend their time on the written answers that win contracts, instead of chasing certificates the week a bid is due.

Checked against GOV.UK guidance on 17 November 2026. Every tender sets its own requirements, so always check the documents for the one you are bidding for. This is general information, not legal advice.

Registration

  • Central Digital Platform. Suppliers bidding under the Procurement Act 2023 need to register on the Central Digital Platform, the enhanced Find a Tender service. It is free, and the core company information you enter there is reused across bids.
  • The buyer's e-tendering portal. Many councils and housing associations run their tenders through their own portal. Registering is free, but do it well before the deadline: some send login details by email and occasionally need manual approval.

Insurance

  • Employer's liability. A legal requirement if you employ anyone, normally with cover of at least £5 million.
  • Public liability. The level is set by the buyer. £5 million and £10 million are both common for building work.
  • Professional indemnity. Usually asked for only where you take on design responsibility, such as design-and-build or mechanical and electrical design.

Keep current certificates as PDFs, and note the renewal dates: an expired certificate is one of the easiest ways to fail a pass or fail check.

Health and safety

  • A health and safety policy, signed and dated, with your arrangements and who is responsible.
  • Accreditation from a scheme in the Safety Schemes in Procurement (SSIP) forum, such as CHAS or SafeContractor. Many buyers accept SSIP membership in place of detailed health and safety questions.
  • Accident and enforcement history for the last three years: RIDDOR-reportable incidents and any Health and Safety Executive notices.
  • Example risk assessments and method statements from a recent job.
  • CSCS cards and trade qualifications for the people who will do the work.

Financial standing

  • Two or three years of accounts. Buyers use them to check that the contract is a sensible size for your business.
  • A bank reference or credit check. Some buyers run their own check through a credit agency instead.

Experience

  • Two or three case studies of similar recent work, each with the client, value, dates, scope and outcome.
  • Referees who have agreed to be contacted, ideally from public-sector clients.

Policies and certifications

Which of these you need depends on the size and type of contract:

  • Quality, environmental and safety management. ISO 9001, ISO 14001 and ISO 45001 are often scored, or requested on larger contracts, but are rarely essential for small works.
  • Equality and diversity, and safeguarding. Safeguarding policies matter for work in schools, care homes and occupied housing.
  • Data protection. A short policy covering how you handle residents' and staff details.
  • Cyber Essentials. Required for some central government contracts that involve handling personal data or IT systems.
  • A Carbon Reduction Plan. Required for central government contracts worth more than £5 million a year, including VAT, under PPN 006. Some other public bodies ask for one, or for a net zero commitment, on smaller contracts.
  • A modern slavery statement. A legal requirement only for businesses with a turnover of £36 million or more, though buyers may ask smaller firms how they check their supply chain.

Pre-qualification for construction: PAS 91

PAS 91 is the standard set of pre-qualification questions for construction work. If you have answered it once, through a scheme like Constructionline or for a previous tender, keep your answers: many buyers ask the same questions. For contracts below the procurement thresholds, the Procurement Act stops buyers running a separate pre-qualification stage before you can bid.

Keep it in one place

Set up one folder with every document above, each named clearly with its expiry date where it has one, and update it every quarter. When a tender appears, you can go straight to the bid itself. Our guide on how small construction firms win public-sector work covers what to do next.

How small construction firms win public-sector work

Public bodies spend billions a year on building work, repairs and maintenance, and much of it goes to small firms. Winning it is less about luck or connections than about choosing the right tenders, having your paperwork ready, and answering the question the buyer actually asked.

Checked against GOV.UK guidance and the Procurement Act 2023 on 17 November 2026. This is general information, not legal advice.

Small firms are not an afterthought

Under the Procurement Act 2023, a small or medium-sized enterprise (SME) is a business with fewer than 250 staff and either a turnover under £44 million or a balance sheet of £38 million or less. That covers almost every local builder, roofer, electrician and maintenance contractor.

The Act puts duties on buyers to think about SMEs. For contracts below the procurement thresholds, a buyer must consider the barriers small firms face and take proportionate steps to reduce them, and it cannot run a separate pre-qualification stage that filters suppliers out before they bid. Buyers can also reserve below-threshold competitions for SMEs, for voluntary and community organisations, or for local suppliers.

The early data is encouraging. Analysis of the Act's first year by the Open Contracting Partnership found that at least one SME won 61% of contracts awarded through open tenders, rising to 73% in open competitions below the thresholds.

1. Pick contracts you can win

The most common mistake is bidding for everything. A firm with a £2 million turnover is unlikely to win a £5 million contract, however good its written answers, because buyers check that a contract is a sensible share of your turnover. Concentrate on:

  • Contracts sized to your business. Buyers often test a contract's annual value against your turnover, for example by setting a minimum turnover. The tender documents state the exact test, so check it before you start.
  • Local work. Councils, schools and housing associations within a sensible travel distance value local knowledge and quick response times, and may reserve some below-threshold work for local firms.
  • Lots. Larger contracts and frameworks are often split into lots by type of work, value or area. A single local lot is usually a better bet than the whole contract.
  • Your trade. Specialist contracts in roofing, mechanical and electrical, or groundworks attract fewer bidders than general building contracts.

Noticehawk scores each tender out of 100 partly on this basis: how well the value, location and type of work fit a smaller firm.

2. Get registered and get your paperwork ready

Since 24 February 2025, suppliers bidding under the Procurement Act need to be registered on the Central Digital Platform, the enhanced Find a Tender service. Registration is free, and you store your core company details once and reuse them for every bid.

Beyond registration, most buyers will ask for the same set of documents: insurance certificates, health and safety evidence, accounts, policies and references. Gathering these before a tender lands saves days when the deadline is tight. Our paperwork checklist lists what is usually asked for.

3. Decide quickly whether to bid

A good tender response takes time, so make a bid or no-bid decision within a day or two of finding a tender. Ask:

  • Do we meet every pass or fail requirement: insurance levels, accreditations, turnover?
  • Can we deliver it alongside our existing work, at the price we would need to charge?
  • Do we have recent, similar projects to point to as evidence?
  • Is there an incumbent who has held the contract for years? That is not a reason to walk away, but it raises the bar.
  • Have we got enough time before the deadline to write a strong response?

4. Answer what the buyer asked

Most construction tenders are scored on a mix of quality and price, and since February 2025 many public bodies also score social value. The tender documents tell you exactly how each answer will be marked. Read the scoring guide before writing a word, answer every part of every question, and back each claim with evidence from a real project. Our guide to writing a tender response that scores well goes through this step by step.

5. Learn from every result

Under the Procurement Act, buyers must send an assessment summary when a contract is awarded through a competitive tendering procedure. Unsuccessful bidders see their own scores alongside the winning bidder's. Read it carefully: it shows exactly where you lost marks, and it is the single best way to improve your next bid.

6. Get paid on time

Public contracts carry 30-day payment terms, and the Act implies 30-day terms into below-threshold contracts too. Those terms are meant to flow down the supply chain, which also helps if you subcontract to a larger contractor on public work.

Where to start this week

  • Register on the Central Digital Platform at Find a Tender, if you have not already.
  • Put your insurance certificates, health and safety evidence and last two years' accounts in one folder.
  • Write three short case studies of recent jobs, with the client, value, what you did and the outcome.
  • Set up alerts for your region and type of work, so you see tenders on the day they are published rather than a week before they close.

How Noticehawk works

Where the tenders come from, how often they are checked, how each score is worked out, and what the service does not do.

Where the tenders come from

Every tender comes from one of three official notice services: Find a Tender, which carries higher-value contracts from across the UK; Contracts Finder, which carries lower-value contracts, mostly in England; and Public Contracts Scotland, where Scottish public bodies must advertise. All three publish under the Open Government Licence v3.0. Every tender we send links to its official notice.

We keep notices for construction, building maintenance, building services and design work, and leave the rest out.

How often it is checked

All three services are read every morning. Alerts go out by email every Monday, and on paid plans from Tuesday to Friday whenever something new matches, and the tender pages on this website are rebuilt every day.

Values in the buyer and market pages come from award notices; where one framework total is repeated against every firm appointed, we leave it out of the typical values, because it is a ceiling for the whole framework and not what any one firm was paid.

Changed, withdrawn and closed notices

  • Changed. When a buyer amends a notice, the next morning's check picks up the new details, such as a moved closing date, and the tender is scored again.
  • Withdrawn or cancelled. These are taken out of alerts and off this website at the next morning's check.
  • Closed. A tender drops out once its closing date has passed. Alerts only include tenders with at least a few days left to bid.
  • Published twice. Some contracts appear on more than one service. We show them once.

A notice can change after an alert has gone out, so always check the official notice for the current closing date and time before you start a bid.

How the type of work is decided

Each notice is first sorted by its procurement codes and wording. The label is then checked each day using an AI model, which corrects the type of work where the rules got it wrong and removes notices that are not construction or building maintenance at all. Tenders on this website have all been through that check.

How the score is worked out

Each tender gets a score out of 100 from a fixed points table. Nothing in the score is a guess about your firm; it measures how well the tender suits a smaller construction business in general.

  • Fit with construction work, up to 30 points. Highest when both the procurement code and the wording say it is construction or maintenance work.
  • Contract size, up to 25 points. Highest when the stated value is between £100,000 and £500,000.
  • Time to bid, up to 20 points. Highest when there are three to five weeks left.
  • Suits smaller firms, up to 15 points. Highest when the buyer says it is suitable for smaller firms; some points if it is split into lots.
  • Detail in the notice, 10 points. Given when the notice states the value, the closing date and where the work is.

Paid alerts show these points for every tender, together with anything the notice leaves out and anything worth checking first, such as a short deadline or a framework that does not guarantee work.

Scores for your business, not a typical one

The score above is for a typical smaller firm. On paid plans you can tell us about yours, and your scores, the order of your tenders and what we send all change to match:

  • Contract size. The smallest job worth bidding for and the largest you would take on.
  • Where you work. Your base postcode and how far you travel. Tenders beyond that score lower and say how far they are.
  • What you specialise in. A tender that mentions one of your specialisms scores higher.
  • What to leave out. Work you never take, such as asbestos, and frameworks if you only want direct contracts.

Each change is explained on the tender, so you can see why it moved. You can also mark any tender "Not for me".

AI assist: help with the bid

On paid plans, every tender in your account has an AI assist button, and every tender in your alert email links to it. It sets out:

  • Key dates. When bids close, the deadline for questions, and when to have your price finished.
  • What the buyer states (Professional and Premium). Insurance levels, accreditations, minimum turnover, how bids are scored, the form of contract and any site visit, taken from the notice and from documents the buyer has published openly.
  • What to have ready. A checklist you tick off. Your ticks are remembered on your device, so the next tender shows only what is missing.
  • Where the marks are, and questions worth asking the buyer.
  • A check of your draft. Paste your written answers and it shows which checklist items it cannot find, where an insurance level is not stated, and where claims have no evidence. The check runs on your own device; your draft is not sent to us.

It is a starting point, not a bid-writing service. The buyer's tender pack is the full statement of what is required. See which plan includes what.

Subcontract work board

Contractors can post work they want priced, free of charge, and firms reply to them directly. A post appears once the contractor confirms their email address, and comes down on its closing date. See the board.

What Noticehawk does not do

  • It reads the published notice, not the full tender pack. Requirements such as accreditations, insurance levels and minimum turnover are usually set out in the buyer's documents, which sit on the buyer's own portal. Where a notice does state them, paid alerts say so.
  • It does not tell you whether your firm is eligible, and a high score is not a prediction that you will win.
  • It does not write or submit bids. AI assist tells you what a bid needs; the writing and the price are yours.
  • Coverage of lower-value contracts in Wales and Northern Ireland is thinner, because those are mostly published on their own notice services, which do not yet offer data we can read. Many small Scottish contracts are let by invitation (Quick Quotes) and never published at all.
